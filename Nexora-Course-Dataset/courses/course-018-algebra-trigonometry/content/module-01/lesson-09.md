###  Key Concepts  
  
### [ 1.1 Real Numbers: Algebra Essentials ](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>)

  * Rational numbers may be written as fractions or terminating or repeating decimals. See [Example 1](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>) and [Example 2](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>).
  * Determine whether a number is rational or irrational by writing it as a decimal. See [Example 3](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>).
  * The rational numbers and irrational numbers make up the set of real numbers. See [Example 4](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>). A number can be classified as natural, whole, integer, rational, or irrational. See [Example 5](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>).
  * The order of operations is used to evaluate expressions. See [Example 6](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>).
  * The real numbers under the operations of addition and multiplication obey basic rules, known as the properties of real numbers. These are the commutative properties, the associative properties, the distributive property, the identity properties, and the inverse properties. See [Example 7](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>).
  * Algebraic expressions are composed of constants and variables that are combined using addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. See [Example 8](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>). They take on a numerical value when evaluated by replacing variables with constants. See [Example 9](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>), [Example 10](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>), and [Example 12](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>)
  * Formulas are equations in which one quantity is represented in terms of other quantities. They may be simplified or evaluated as any mathematical expression. See [Example 11](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>) and [Example 13](<1-1-real-numbers-algebra-essentials>).

### [ 1.2 Exponents and Scientific Notation ](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>)

  * Products of exponential expressions with the same base can be simplified by adding exponents. See [Example 1](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * Quotients of exponential expressions with the same base can be simplified by subtracting exponents. See [Example 2](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * Powers of exponential expressions with the same base can be simplified by multiplying exponents. See [Example 3](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * An expression with exponent zero is defined as 1. See [Example 4](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * An expression with a negative exponent is defined as a reciprocal. See [Example 5](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>) and [Example 6](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * The power of a product of factors is the same as the product of the powers of the same factors. See [Example 7](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * The power of a quotient of factors is the same as the quotient of the powers of the same factors. See [Example 8](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * The rules for exponential expressions can be combined to simplify more complicated expressions. See [Example 9](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * Scientific notation uses powers of 10 to simplify very large or very small numbers. See [Example 10](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>) and [Example 11](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).
  * Scientific notation may be used to simplify calculations with very large or very small numbers. See [Example 12](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>) and [Example 13](<1-2-exponents-and-scientific-notation>).

### [ 1.3 Radicals and Rational Exponents ](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>)

  * The principal square root of a number  a a is the nonnegative number that when multiplied by itself equals  a. a. See [Example 1](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>).
  * If  a a and  b b are nonnegative, the square root of the product  ab ab is equal to the product of the square roots of  a a and  b b See [Example 2](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>) and [Example 3](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>).
  * If  a a and  b b are nonnegative, the square root of the quotient  a b a b is equal to the quotient of the square roots of  a a and  b b See [Example 4](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>) and [Example 5](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>).
  * We can add and subtract radical expressions if they have the same radicand and the same index. See [Example 6](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>) and [Example 7](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>).
  * Radical expressions written in simplest form do not contain a radical in the denominator. To eliminate the square root radical from the denominator, multiply both the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate of the denominator. See [Example 8](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>) and [Example 9](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>).
  * The principal _n_ th root of  a a is the number with the same sign as  a a that when raised to the _n_ th power equals  a. a. These roots have the same properties as square roots.********See[Example 10](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>).
  * Radicals can be rewritten as rational exponents and rational exponents can be rewritten as radicals. See [Example 11](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>) and [Example 12](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>).
  * The properties of exponents apply to rational exponents. See [Example 13](<1-3-radicals-and-rational-exponents>).

### [ 1.4 Polynomials ](<1-4-polynomials>)

  * A polynomial is a sum of terms each consisting of a variable raised to a non-negative integer power. The degree is the highest power of the variable that occurs in the polynomial. The leading term is the term containing the highest degree, and the leading coefficient is the coefficient of that term. See [Example 1](<1-4-polynomials>).
  * We can add and subtract polynomials by combining like terms. See [Example 2](<1-4-polynomials>) and [Example 3](<1-4-polynomials>).
  * To multiply polynomials, use the distributive property to multiply each term in the first polynomial by each term in the second. Then add the products. See [Example 4](<1-4-polynomials>).
  * FOIL (First, Outer, Inner, Last) is a shortcut that can be used to multiply binomials. See [Example 5](<1-4-polynomials>).
  * Perfect square trinomials and difference of squares are special products. See [Example 6](<1-4-polynomials>) and [Example 7](<1-4-polynomials>).
  * Follow the same rules to work with polynomials containing several variables. See [Example 8](<1-4-polynomials>).

### [ 1.5 Factoring Polynomials ](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>)

  * The greatest common factor, or GCF, can be factored out of a polynomial. Checking for a GCF should be the first step in any factoring problem. See [Example 1](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>).
  * Trinomials with leading coefficient 1 can be factored by finding numbers that have a product of the third term and a sum of the second term. See [Example 2](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>).
  * Trinomials can be factored using a process called factoring by grouping. See [Example 3](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>).
  * Perfect square trinomials and the difference of squares are special products and can be factored using equations. See [Example 4](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>) and [Example 5](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>).
  * The sum of cubes and the difference of cubes can be factored using equations. See [Example 6](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>) and [Example 7](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>).
  * Polynomials containing fractional and negative exponents can be factored by pulling out a GCF. See [Example 8](<1-5-factoring-polynomials>).

### [ 1.6 Rational Expressions ](<1-6-rational-expressions>)

  * Rational expressions can be simplified by cancelling common factors in the numerator and denominator. See [Example 1](<1-6-rational-expressions>).
  * We can multiply rational expressions by multiplying the numerators and multiplying the denominators. See [Example 2](<1-6-rational-expressions>).
  * To divide rational expressions, multiply by the reciprocal of the second expression. See [Example 3](<1-6-rational-expressions>).
  * Adding or subtracting rational expressions requires finding a common denominator. See [Example 4](<1-6-rational-expressions>) and [Example 5](<1-6-rational-expressions>).
  * Complex rational expressions have fractions in the numerator or the denominator. These expressions can be simplified. See [Example 6](<1-6-rational-expressions>).
