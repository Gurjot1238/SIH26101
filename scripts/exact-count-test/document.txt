The Consumer Price Index measures the change in prices paid by households for a fixed basket of goods and services over time. The current index uses 2012 as its base year, and every published value is expressed against that base year. Price collection covers 1181 selected markets across the country, and trained field staff record quoted prices on a fixed schedule every single month. The all-India inflation rate reported for the reference month was 7.2 percent, while the corresponding rural figure stood at 6.4 percent. The National Statistical Office compiles these figures and publishes them under a strict release calendar that is announced well in advance.

The basket weights are derived from the Consumer Expenditure Survey, which records how households actually allocate their spending across categories. Food and beverages carry the largest weight in the combined index at 45 percent, followed by housing at 10 percent and fuel at 7 percent. When a surveyed item becomes unavailable in a market, the field officer selects a comparable replacement of similar quality so that the price series remains continuous. This substitution procedure protects the index from spurious jumps that would otherwise arise from missing observations.

The Periodic Labour Force Survey estimates employment and unemployment using the current weekly status approach and the usual status approach. A person is classified as employed under the current weekly status if they worked for at least one hour on any day during the reference week. The survey adopts a rotational panel design in urban areas, where each selected household is visited across four consecutive quarters. The reference quarter for the latest bulletin was the quarter ending in December, and the urban unemployment rate for that reference quarter was measured at 6.8 percent.

Sampling error arises because an estimate is computed from a sample rather than from a complete census of the population. The National Sample Survey Office publishes the estimated standard error alongside each major indicator so that users can judge its reliability. A larger sample size reduces the sampling error but raises the cost and the time required for data collection. Non-sampling error, by contrast, includes mistakes in measurement, coverage gaps, and non-response, and it does not shrink automatically as the sample grows larger.

Data dissemination follows the principles set out in the Special Data Dissemination Standard maintained by the International Monetary Fund. Under these principles the release calendar is published ahead of time, revisions are documented transparently, and the methodology is made available to every user. The response rate for the latest urban survey round was 88 percent, and the office treats any round falling below its stated threshold as requiring a documented quality review before release.

The Gross Domestic Product estimates are compiled by the National Accounts Division using both the production approach and the expenditure approach. The base year for the current national accounts series is 2011, and the provisional annual growth estimate released for the year was 7.8 percent. Quarterly estimates are revised three times as fuller administrative data becomes available, and the first revision typically arrives within two months of the initial release. The deflator used to convert nominal values into real values is derived from the same price indices that underpin the retail inflation series.

The Annual Survey of Industries covers registered factories employing ten or more workers with power, or twenty or more workers without power. The frame for the survey is drawn from the Business Register maintained by the Chief Inspector of Factories in each state. The latest survey achieved a coverage of 94 percent of the sampled factories, and the value of output was collected for every reporting unit in the census sector. Small units below the employment threshold fall outside the frame and are instead captured by the unincorporated enterprise surveys conducted separately.

Seasonal adjustment removes recurring calendar patterns from a time series so that the underlying trend becomes visible. The office applies an established decomposition method that separates the trend component, the seasonal component, and the irregular component. A festival that shifts between months from one year to the next introduces a moving-holiday effect that the adjustment model corrects explicitly. Without such adjustment, a routine seasonal rise in food prices before a major festival could be mistaken for a genuine acceleration in underlying inflation.
